================================================================
CHAPTER 1: WHAT IS INFERENTIAL STATISTICS?
================================================================

Inferential statistics uses data from a sample to draw conclusions about a larger
population. Because it is rarely practical to measure an entire population, we
collect a representative sample and use statistical methods to make inferences.

Key distinction:
  - Descriptive statistics: summarises the data you actually have (mean, median,
    standard deviation, charts). It does not go beyond the observed data.
  - Inferential statistics: uses the sample to make statements about the population,
    with quantified uncertainty. Examples: hypothesis tests, confidence intervals.

Population: the complete group we want to study.
Sample: a subset of the population that we actually observe.
Parameter: a numerical characteristic of the population (e.g., the true mean μ).
Statistic: a numerical characteristic of the sample (e.g., the sample mean x̄).

================================================================
CHAPTER 2: SAMPLING
================================================================

Simple Random Sample: every individual in the population has an equal chance
of being selected. This minimizes selection bias.

Stratified Sample: the population is divided into subgroups (strata) and a
random sample is drawn from each stratum. Ensures all subgroups are represented.

Systematic Sample: select every k-th individual from a list.

Convenience Sample: select whoever is easiest to reach. This often introduces
bias and should be avoided for formal inference.

Sampling Bias: when the sample is not representative of the population,
conclusions drawn from it may not hold for the population.

Central Limit Theorem (CLT): if we draw many independent random samples of
size n from any population with a finite mean μ and standard deviation σ,
the distribution of sample means will approach a normal distribution as n
increases, regardless of the shape of the original population.

Implication: for sufficiently large samples (commonly n ≥ 30), we can use
normal-distribution methods even when the population is not normally distributed.

================================================================
CHAPTER 3: HYPOTHESIS TESTING
================================================================

A hypothesis test evaluates whether sample data provides enough evidence to
reject a default assumption about the population.

The Null Hypothesis (H₀): the default assumption — often a statement of
"no effect", "no difference", or "no relationship". We assume H₀ is true
until evidence forces us to reject it.

The Alternative Hypothesis (H₁ or Hₐ): the claim we are testing — the effect,
difference, or relationship we want to detect.

Examples:
  H₀: The new drug has no effect on blood pressure (mean change = 0).
  H₁: The new drug does change blood pressure (mean change ≠ 0).

  H₀: The mean exam score of Group A equals the mean of Group B.
  H₁: The mean exam score of Group A is greater than that of Group B.

The Logic of Hypothesis Testing:
  1. Assume H₀ is true.
  2. Calculate how likely it is to observe our sample result (or something more extreme)
     if H₀ were true.
  3. If this probability is very small, the data are inconsistent with H₀, so we
     reject H₀.
  4. If this probability is not very small, we fail to reject H₀ (we do NOT say
     we "accept" H₀ — absence of evidence is not evidence of absence).

================================================================
CHAPTER 4: THE P-VALUE
================================================================

The p-value is the probability of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme
as the one observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true.

  p-value = P(data this extreme OR MORE extreme | H₀ is true)

How to interpret the p-value:
  - Small p-value (e.g., p < 0.05): the observed result would be unlikely if H₀
    were true. This is considered statistically significant evidence against H₀.
    We reject H₀.
  - Large p-value (e.g., p ≥ 0.05): the observed result is not unusual if H₀
    were true. We fail to reject H₀.

The significance level α is the threshold we set in advance (before seeing the data):
  - Common choices: α = 0.05, α = 0.01, α = 0.10.
  - If p ≤ α, we reject H₀.
  - If p > α, we fail to reject H₀.

Critical misconceptions about p-values:
  - The p-value is NOT the probability that H₀ is true.
  - The p-value is NOT the probability of making a mistake.
  - Statistical significance does NOT imply practical importance.
  - A large p-value does NOT mean H₀ is true — only that there is insufficient
    evidence to reject it.

================================================================
CHAPTER 5: TYPE I AND TYPE II ERRORS
================================================================

Errors in hypothesis testing:

  Type I Error (False Positive): we reject H₀ when H₀ is actually true.
    Probability of Type I error = α (the significance level).
    Example: concluding a drug works when it actually has no effect.

  Type II Error (False Negative): we fail to reject H₀ when H₁ is actually true.
    Probability of Type II error = β.
    Example: concluding a drug has no effect when it actually does work.

Power of a test: the probability of correctly rejecting H₀ when H₁ is true.
    Power = 1 − β

Increasing sample size increases power (reduces β) without changing α.
There is a trade-off: decreasing α (being more conservative about rejecting H₀)
increases β (making it harder to detect real effects).

================================================================
CHAPTER 6: CONFIDENCE INTERVALS
================================================================

A confidence interval provides a range of plausible values for a population
parameter, with a stated level of confidence.

A 95% confidence interval for a population mean is computed as:
    x̄ ± z * (σ / √n)

where:
  x̄   = sample mean
  z    = critical value from the standard normal distribution (z = 1.96 for 95%)
  σ    = population standard deviation (or sample std s if σ is unknown)
  n    = sample size

Interpretation: if we repeated this procedure many times, 95% of the intervals
constructed this way would contain the true population mean.

IMPORTANT: A 95% CI does NOT mean there is a 95% probability that the true
mean lies in this specific interval. The true mean is fixed; the interval
is random. After computing, the interval either contains the true mean or it does not.

Relationship to hypothesis testing:
  - If a 95% CI does not contain the null value, the two-sided test rejects H₀
    at α = 0.05.
  - A narrower CI indicates more precision (larger sample or less variance).
  - A wider CI indicates more uncertainty.

================================================================
CHAPTER 7: COMMON STATISTICAL TESTS
================================================================

One-sample t-test: tests whether a sample mean equals a hypothesised value.
  Use when: comparing sample mean to a known standard.

Two-sample t-test (independent): tests whether two independent group means differ.
  Use when: comparing outcomes between two different groups.

Paired t-test: tests whether the mean difference within pairs is zero.
  Use when: the same subjects are measured before and after an intervention.

Chi-squared test of independence: tests whether two categorical variables are
related or independent.
  Use when: both variables are categorical (e.g., gender and voting preference).

ANOVA (Analysis of Variance): tests whether means differ across three or more groups.
  Use when: comparing more than two group means.

Assumptions that must be checked:
  - t-tests assume approximate normality (or large n by CLT) and homogeneity of variance.
  - Chi-squared test requires adequate expected cell frequencies (≥ 5 in each cell).
  - All tests assume random sampling.

================================================================
CHAPTER 8: CORRELATION AND REGRESSION IN INFERENTIAL STATISTICS
================================================================

Pearson Correlation Coefficient (r): measures the linear relationship between
two continuous variables. Ranges from −1 (perfect negative) to +1 (perfect positive).
r = 0 indicates no linear relationship.

Testing whether r equals zero:
  H₀: ρ = 0 (no linear relationship in the population)
  H₁: ρ ≠ 0 (a linear relationship exists)
  A t-test is used to evaluate this hypothesis.

Correlation does NOT imply causation. Two variables can be correlated because:
  - One causes the other.
  - Both are caused by a third variable (confounding).
  - The correlation is coincidental.

Simple Linear Regression (inference):
  - We test whether the population slope β₁ equals zero.
  - H₀: β₁ = 0 (the predictor has no linear effect on the outcome)
  - H₁: β₁ ≠ 0 (the predictor has a linear effect)
  - The p-value for the slope tells us whether the predictor is statistically significant.

R-squared (R²) in regression: the proportion of variance in the outcome variable
explained by the predictor(s). R² = 0.80 means 80% of the variance in y is
accounted for by the model.
